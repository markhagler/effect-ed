Lesson 1

Waiting Is Normal

JavaScript is a kitchen with exactly one cook. Lots of things in a program involve waiting: for a file, for the internet, for a timer. If the cook stands there staring at the oven, nothing else gets done. So JavaScript has a trick for waiting without freezing.

The story

Picture a food truck with one cook, Sam. An order comes in: toast and a smoothie.

The wrong way. Sam puts bread in the toaster and stands in front of it, arms crossed, for two minutes. The truck looks frozen.
The right way. The toaster gets a bell. Sam says "ring when you're done," walks away, and starts the smoothie.
Ding! Sam comes back for the toast. Sam never did two things at the same instant. Sam just never stood still.

That bell is the whole secret. The cook says: "Toaster, when you're done, ring the bell and I'll come back." In code, the "come back and do this" part is called a callback.

The picture

How does Sam know the bell rang? Sam has a ticket rail. Every time a bell rings, a ticket goes on the rail: "toast is ready, go butter it." Whenever Sam's hands are free, Sam takes the next ticket. Programmers call this ticket rail the event loop. You don't need to remember that name today. You need to remember: one cook, a rail of tickets, hands never idle.

The tiniest code

console.log("1. put bread in")

setTimeout(() => {
  console.log("3. 🔔 toast ready")   // this runs LATER, when the bell rings
}, 2000)

console.log("2. make smoothie")     // this runs RIGHT AWAY, Sam didn't wait

Run it and the numbers come out in order: 1, 2, then 3 two seconds later. The function inside setTimeout is the ticket Sam left for later. That's a callback.

The freeze test

The ball bounces and the counter ticks as long as Sam's hands are free. Make toast both ways and watch what happens to them. The blocking button really freezes this page for two seconds. That's the point.

Check yourself

  
1. Sam is one person. Did Sam ever do two things at the exact same moment?No. Sam did one thing, then another, then another. The toaster did its own work, but the toaster isn't Sam. One cook, one thing at a time, always.

  
2. Why does line "2" print before line "3", even though "3" is written above it?Line 3 is inside the ticket Sam left for later. Sam keeps going down the list (prints 2), and only comes back for the ticket when the bell rings two seconds later.

  
3. A web page freezes when you click a button. In kitchen words, what did the programmer do wrong?They made Sam stand and stare at something instead of leaving a ticket. While Sam stares, no other ticket gets done, so the page can't even redraw itself.

  
4. If Sam gets three bells at once, what happens?Three tickets go on the rail. Sam does them one at a time, in the order they arrived.


  

    Teacher notes
    
    
      
  • The word for "the cook stops and stares" is synchronous or blocking. The word for "leave a ticket, come back later" is asynchronous. Introduce those words only after the story is solid.
    • 
      
  • Resist saying "multithreading." JavaScript in the browser and in Node has one cook. Fibers (Lesson 6) will feel like cheating later, and it's worth having the one-cook rule firmly set first.
    • 
      
  • Callbacks get messy when you need "when this is done, do that, and when that is done, do this." Show a three-deep nested setTimeout on the board and ask if anyone wants to read it. That mess is why Lesson 2 exists.
    • 
      
  • The freeze demo uses a real busy-wait loop. Kids will ask why the ball stops but the toaster still "finishes." Answer: the ball is drawn by Sam. The toaster timer is not Sam.
